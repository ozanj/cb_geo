# Social Problems Revision Memo: Geomarkets / College Board

## Purpose of this memo

This is the living project memo for revising the Geomarkets / College Board manuscript after the Sociology of Education desk rejection, with **Social Problems** as the likely target. It is meant to preserve the conceptual decisions, section architecture, evidence bank, and open questions that emerge during revision work.

Treat this memo as the **authoritative working record** of decisions we have already made. Update it as the argument develops.

---

## 1. Forest-level diagnosis

Laura Hamilton’s advice is useful at the **forest level**, not necessarily as a set of tree-level instructions.

The main problem with the submitted version is that it reads too much like a paper about **what Geomarkets do**. The revised paper should instead use Geomarkets to illuminate a more general sociological process:

> **How does existing social inequality become translated into classifications that organizations can act upon?**

The paper should speak to broader sociological interests in:

- social structure and organizational action;
- embeddedness and markets;
- classification;
- intermediaries;
- organizational knowledge;
- market devices;
- social reproduction.

The core move is to treat **market devices as the hinge between social structure and organizational action**.

---

## 2. Core mechanism

The working processual argument is:

> **stratified social geography → elite-college organizational perception/culture → formalization of that knowledge → College Board as intermediary → proprietary commercial classification → organizational affordances/action**

This is the main conceptual backbone of the revision.

### Important clarification

Geomarkets do **not** create residential racial/class inequality.

Rather:

1. residential geography is already racially and socioeconomically stratified;
2. actors in elite college admissions perceive and interpret that geography through a shared organizational culture;
3. Zemsky and Oedel formalize that knowledge into classifications and models;
4. College Board standardizes, commodifies, and scales those classifications;
5. College Board products make those classifications available as routine organizational choices;
6. colleges can then act through those categories.

The strongest concise formulation so far is:

> **A socially situated way of seeing unequal social space becomes standardized infrastructure for acting on that space.**

Or:

> **Social relations shape the production of devices; devices subsequently shape organizational action.**

---

## 3. Elite-college genealogy: settled interpretation

### What is empirically warranted

The evidence supports an **elite-college** or **elite/selective-private-college** genealogy.

The evidence currently does **not** warrant calling this a **Protestant** genealogy.

Use “elite colleges,” “selective private colleges,” or “elite/selective private colleges.”

### Why COFHE matters

COFHE is not incidental background.

The Zemsky/Oedel project emerges from:

- University of Pennsylvania;
- the Market Research Committee of the Consortium on Financing Higher Education (COFHE);
- a network described by Zemsky and Oedel as roughly thirty selective private institutions;
- an established practice of sharing applicant-pool and market information.

The key claim is:

> The originating classification was developed within a network of elite private colleges before College Board helped generalize it into infrastructure.

This genealogy should be **folded into the main mechanism**, not treated as a separate empirical project.

The role of the elite-college genealogy is to answer:

> **Whose way of seeing the market was being formalized?**

# THEORY SUBSECTION 1: MARKET DEVICES

## 5. Expository arc

The theoretical exposition should proceed in this order:

### A. Embeddedness / ongoing social relations

Start with the economic-sociology claim that economic action is embedded in ongoing social relations rather than carried out by atomized actors.

This situates the paper in relation to Granovetter-style embeddedness scholarship.

### B. Market-devices intervention

Then introduce the market-devices / performativity intervention:

Social relations alone do not explain how actors perceive markets, calculate, classify, compare, or act. Material and calculative arrangements also matter.

Market devices can include:

- classifications;
- metrics;
- rankings;
- interfaces;
- databases;
- algorithms;
- search filters;
- software tools.

Market devices help make economic action possible.

### C. Key concepts

The concepts that probably need to appear are:

#### Market devices
Tools and arrangements that classify, calculate, organize, and make markets actionable.

#### Affordances
The practical actions a device makes possible, easy, or routine.

#### Framing
How the device defines what is visible, eligible, comparable, selectable, and meaningful for action.

#### Agencement
Action is produced through configurations of actors, devices, organizational goals, routines, incentives, and institutions. Neither the device nor the organization acts in isolation.

#### Performativity — optional
Use only if it earns its keep. It may be useful for the idea that devices can help reproduce or reshape the social order they describe, but it should not become a major detour.

### D. Return to the social origins of devices

Then circle back:

Market devices themselves do not fall from the sky. They are produced within socially embedded settings.

This is where **MacKenzie and Millo** become especially useful.

Their work helps bridge:

- embeddedness;
- cultures / moral communities;
- collective action;
- technical and calculative devices;
- performativity.

The theoretical synthesis to preserve is:

> **Social embeddedness helps explain where devices come from; market-device theory helps explain what devices subsequently do.**

### Important caution

Do **not** make “devices originate in densely connected, culturally homogeneous groups” into a general law attributed to Granovetter, MacKenzie, or market-devices scholarship.

The general proposition should be broader:

> Devices emerge within socially embedded networks, cultures, institutions, and collective projects.

Then the **Geomarkets case** provides a particularly strong empirical example of a dense, culturally similar elite-college community.

---

## 6. Deep logic versus expository order

Keep this distinction clear.

### Deep causal/process logic

> **social relations shape the production of devices → devices subsequently shape organizational action**

### Expository order in theory subsection 1

1. social relations matter;
2. devices also matter;
3. devices themselves emerge from social relations.

This is the preferred rhetorical order.

# THEORY SUBSECTION 2: APPLYING THEORY TO GEOMARKETS

## 7. Broad application arc

The application subsection should be **asymmetric on purpose**.

The upstream genealogy is important, but the downstream affordance/action side should receive more weight because that is where the manuscript’s main empirical analyses are located.

### Step 1. Start with preexisting stratified geography

Residential geography is already stratified by race and class.

Geomarkets do not create this structure.

The **1980 Census Geomarket maps** can provide a visual/evidentiary anchor here because they show the relevant social structure at approximately the same historical moment when Zemsky and Oedel were building the classifications.

Possible theoretical move:

> Before Geomarkets became a market device, the metropolitan spaces they partitioned were already sharply differentiated by race and socioeconomic status.

Important distinction:

- the maps establish the **objective stratified geography**;
- Zemsky/Oedel provide evidence about how admissions professionals **perceived and classified** that geography.

Do not imply that Zemsky/Oedel explicitly conceptualized racial segregation if the evidence only supports a class-centered framing.

### Step 2. Briefly establish socially embedded origins

Show that the classification emerged from Penn/COFHE/selective private colleges and from admissions officers’ shared understandings of student markets.

The theoretical point is:

> The categories embedded in the device had social origins.

### Step 3. Formalization

Zemsky and Oedel explicitly sought to **“capture and quantify”** admissions officers’ knowledge.

They sought to define geographic boundaries in a way consistent with admissions officers’ **“intuitive understanding of student pools.”**

This is the key move from:

> tacit / local organizational knowledge → formal classification

### Step 4. College Board as intermediary

College Board does more than distribute the classification.

It helps turn socially situated organizational knowledge into something:

- standardized;
- proprietary;
- portable;
- scalable;
- purchasable;
- usable by organizations beyond the originating network.

This is the central intermediary claim.

### Step 5. Commercial classification

Geomarkets and the Market Segment Model become embedded in:

- Enrollment Planning Service (EPS);
- Student Search Service.

The classification becomes infrastructure.

### Step 6. Pivot hard to affordances

Once Geomarkets are embedded in College Board products, users do not need to know their history.

They appear as ordinary organizational options.

In Student Search Service, the relevant affordance is:

> colleges can include or exclude prospective students based on predefined Geomarket categories.

This is the bridge to the actual empirical analysis.

### Step 7. Motivate the empirical analyses

The empirical question is **not**:

- Did elite colleges create inequality?
- Did COFHE cause contemporary racial inequality?
- Did College Board determine college behavior?

The empirical question is closer to:

> **What becomes actionable when a historically produced commercial classification is applied to an already stratified social geography?**

The analyses investigate:

- how Geomarkets partition academically similar students;
- where racial/ethnic and first-generation groups are located across those categories;
- how including/excluding Geomarkets changes the visible prospect pool.

### Useful hinge sentence concept

> Once formalized and embedded in College Board products, these categories no longer depended on the admissions community that produced them; they became standardized affordances through which colleges could act on stratified geography.

---

## 8. Balance rule for the application subsection

The elite-college genealogy is sociologically attractive and should be made clearly.

But it should not swamp the manuscript’s actual empirical contribution.

Recommended balance:

### Upstream — relatively brief
- stratified geography;
- elite-college organizational perception/culture;
- formalization;
- College Board intermediary.

### Downstream — central
- proprietary classification;
- affordances;
- organizational action / visibility;
- distributional consequences demonstrated by the empirical analyses.

The elite-college material may require roughly **one substantial paragraph or perhaps a paragraph and a half** in the application subsection.

The **Case section** can carry more of the historical detail.

# THE CASE: GEOMARKETS AND THE MARKET SEGMENT MODEL

## 9. Revised purpose of the Case section

The Case section should do more historical and institutional work than the SOE version, but less theoretical work than the rejected AJS version.

Its job is to:

> **put the historical and organizational evidence on the table that the theory/application section will interpret.**

The Case section should establish facts and chronology.

Avoid repeatedly telling the reader what these facts “mean” in terms of embeddedness, performativity, agencement, etc. Save that interpretation for the theory application.

---

## 10. Revised Case-section arc

### 1. Origins in an elite-college enrollment problem and network

Start with:

- 1978;
- Penn;
- demographic/enrollment concern;
- Zemsky asked who thinks about Penn and what competitors those students consider;
- Zemsky’s work with COFHE’s Market Research Committee;
- COFHE as a group of selective private colleges engaged in collective market research.

Then introduce College Board:

- Penn/COFHE needed data covering institutions and students at scale;
- College Board was simultaneously trying to improve enrollment-planning services;
- the project moved into a Penn/College Board collaboration.

The chronology should therefore be:

> **Penn / COFHE → College Board**

rather than starting with College Board as though the device originated entirely inside the organization.

### 2. Eliciting admissions-officer knowledge

Restore the richer AJS material here.

Especially useful:

- admissions officers as storytellers;
- admissions intuitions as a “remarkably systematic body of knowledge”;
- Zemsky/Oedel’s stated desire to “capture and quantify” this knowledge;
- research based on “listening carefully” to admissions officers.

This is crucial evidence for the socially embedded origin of the classification.

### 3. Formalizing admissions knowledge into geography

The “initial task” was to define boundaries:

> “in a manner consistent with admissions officers’ intuitive understanding of student pools.”

Then explain:

- region;
- state;
- community;
- 143 initial community-based enrollment markets;
- metropolitan divisions often corresponding to inner city, first-ring suburbs, outer-ring suburbs.

These become Geomarkets.

### 4. Explain the Market Segment Model without letting mechanics dominate

Readers need to understand:

- local;
- in-state;
- regional;
- national;
- based on SAT score-sending behavior.

But the mechanics are secondary to the substantive worldview the model formalized.

### 5. Explain the class-stratified theory of student demand

Zemsky/Oedel identify:

- parental education;
- family income;
- scholastic aptitude;
- educational aspirations.

The central substantive conclusion is that student demand varies with social origin.

Affluent, college-educated communities contain disproportionate numbers of students with more geographically expansive college aspirations.

### 6. Preserve the “peer” logic

The AJS material on competition is useful, but it should be presented as part of Zemsky/Oedel’s substantive worldview rather than as a separate Chun/homophily theoretical section.

Important ideas:

- “like institutions” compete for students;
- private selective colleges compete within status-relevant peer groups;
- the socioeconomic hierarchy of colleges maps onto the socioeconomic hierarchy of the communities from which they recruit;
- admissions officers think in terms of peer institutions and peer markets.

This helps establish the elite-college worldview being formalized.

### 7. Move from description to prescription

This is essential.

Zemsky/Oedel do not merely describe college choice.

They recommend where colleges should concentrate scarce recruiting resources.

Selective colleges should target territories containing students predisposed toward institutions like themselves.

Preserve the passage acknowledging that concentrating effort in these markets leads to:

> a “natural reinforcing of the basic socioeconomic patterns” that shaped college choice.

This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence in the book.

### 8. College Board commercialization and scaling through EPS

In 1984:

- the Market Segment Model / analytic outputs become the basis for EPS;
- EPS allows colleges to analyze markets and competitors;
- marketing presents it as identifying where “best prospects” are located;
- College Board expands the classification nationwide;
- Geomarket codes become proprietary;
- EPS sees substantial use.

The AJS evidence on 1995 uptake is useful because it establishes that this was not an obscure product.

### 9. End with Student Search Service

The final move should put the reader at the doorstep of the manuscript’s empirical analysis.

Geomarkets move from:

> analytic/reporting categories in EPS

to:

> actual selectable filters in Student Search Service.

A college can include or exclude prospect profiles based on Geomarket.

Zemsky/Oedel already discuss student-list purchasing strategies that focus selective-college recruiting on affluent Geomarkets.

College Board later markets EPS and Student Search Service as complementary products.

## 17. Market Segment Model

Four segments:

- local;
- in-state;
- regional;
- national.

Defined through SAT score-sending behavior.

Substantive predictors:

- parental education;
- family income;
- scholastic aptitude;
- educational aspirations.

Key substantive takeaway:

> Student demand is patterned by social origin.

Useful Zemsky/Oedel idea:

> Communities with high levels of income and parental education also contain students with higher SATs and more geographically expansive aspirations.

---

## 18. Peer / competition logic

Useful evidence:

- “collegiate competition occurs principally between like institutions”
- the layering of collegiate competition is largely socioeconomic;
- institutional competition reflects the stratified social and economic characteristics of the communities from which colleges draw students;
- admissions officers understand competition as “keeping company with one’s peers.”

Interpretive use:

> Elite colleges’ understandings of student markets were relational and field-specific.

---

## 19. Recruiting prescription

Key recommendation:

Colleges should focus recruiting on Geomarkets with desired socioeconomic compositions in order to reach desired market segments.

Especially important passage:

> “The result is a natural reinforcing of the basic socioeconomic patterns that gave shape in the first place to the structure of college choice.”

Interpretive use:

> The model moves from description of stratification to prescription for action.

---

## 20. College Board commercialization / EPS

Key facts:

- EPS launched in 1984;
- based on Market Segment Model outputs;
- identifies which Geomarkets and high schools colleges should target;
- marketed as software identifying where “best prospects” are likely to be found;
- College Board expanded the system nationwide;
- Geomarket codes became proprietary;
- substantial institutional uptake by the mid-1990s.

Interpretive use:

> College Board turned socially embedded organizational knowledge into standardized, proprietary, scalable infrastructure.

## 21. Student Search Service

Key facts:

- Geomarkets eventually become actual search filters in Student Search Service;
- colleges can include or exclude prospect profiles by Geomarket;
- Zemsky/Oedel themselves anticipate list-buying strategies that target affluent Geomarkets for selective colleges;
- College Board markets EPS as complementary to Student Search Service.

Interpretive use:

# EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS: WHAT IT DOES AND DOES NOT CLAIM

## 22. What the analyses actually demonstrate

The main empirical analyses are not a historical study of COFHE.

They examine what the stabilized device makes possible.

The analyses can show:

- Geomarkets partition academically similar prospects into racially and socioeconomically distinct pools;
- some racial/ethnic and first-generation groups are disproportionately concentrated in particular Geomarkets;
- excluding particular Geomarkets changes the composition of the prospect pool visible to colleges;
- filtering toward affluent territories can disproportionately exclude Black, Hispanic, and first-generation Asian prospects.

---

## 23. Important distinction: affordance versus observed action

Do not overclaim that the counterfactual analyses directly observe organizational decision-making.

Two different empirical objects exist:

### Actual organizational action
Order records show that colleges sometimes actually use Geomarket filters and sometimes include affluent territories while excluding poorer ones.

### Affordance / counterfactual consequences
The main analyses reconstruct what prospect pools would look like under particular inclusion/exclusion choices.

The theory should distinguish:

> **what the device affords**

from:

> **what every college actually does**
